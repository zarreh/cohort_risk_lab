# In plain language

This app takes a synthetic hospital's worth of patients, uses a
statistical model — not a language model — to flag who might benefit from
a care-management programme, checks whether that model is fair across
race, sex, and age before anyone acts on it, and then has a separate AI
assistant gather the evidence a clinician would want to see. It never
makes the call itself — a person always does.

## The five steps

**1. A statistical model scores every patient.** Not the AI assistant —
a calibrated machine-learning model trained on each patient's history of
diagnoses, encounters, and medications. It never sees a patient's race or
ethnicity. It outputs a number between 0 and 1: how likely this patient is
to have high illness burden in the next six months.

**2. The score gets checked for fairness before anyone sees it.** A
single overall accuracy number can hide a model that works well for most
patients and poorly for a few. This app reports accuracy separately for
every demographic group — age, sex, race, insurance type — and shows the
uncertainty range on each number. A group with too few patients to say
anything reliable is labeled "insufficient data," never given a
misleadingly precise number just to fill in a table.

**3. Flagged patients go into a review queue.** Anyone the model scores
above a threshold — chosen to balance "we missed someone who needed help"
against "we spent time on someone who didn't" — lands in a queue for a
clinician to review, highest risk first.

**4. An AI assistant gathers the evidence, but never decides.** When a
clinician opens a case, a second AI system — separate from the risk model
— pulls together *why* the model flagged this patient: which factors drove
the score, what the patient's recent encounters and lab results show, and
whether anything looks missing. This assistant is not allowed to say
whether the patient should be enrolled. That's not a suggestion in its
instructions — the form it fills out has no box to check for a
recommendation, and everything it writes is automatically scanned for
words like "should be enrolled" or "recommend," which would cause it to
be sent back to gather more evidence and try again.

**5. A person makes the call.** The clinician reads the evidence and
decides: enroll, decline, or defer for more information. That decision,
and any notes explaining it, is saved.

## The one finding this app exists to demonstrate

In 2019, researchers found that a real, widely-used healthcare algorithm
had been trained to predict *how much a patient would cost* the health
system — a reasonable-sounding proxy for "how sick is this patient."
The problem: Black patients, at the *same* level of underlying illness,
have historically had less access to care and so cost the system less —
not because they were healthier, but because they got treated less. A
model trained on cost quietly learned to substitute "cost less" for
"less sick," and under-flagged Black patients who needed help just as
much as anyone else.

This app rebuilds that comparison on its synthetic data: the same model,
trained once on illness burden and once on cost, then checked for whether
each version flags different patients by race. Because the synthetic data
generator doesn't naturally include unequal access to care, this app adds
it deliberately — a disclosed, published adjustment, not a hidden bug —
specifically so the comparison has something real to catch. Every page
that shows the result says so plainly. The point being demonstrated is
that checking a model's fairness *per group, not on average* is what
catches this kind of problem — not that this app discovered a new one.

## What this app is not

It is not a medical device. It does not diagnose anyone or screen for a
disease it hasn't already been told the patient has. It runs entirely on
synthetic patients generated by an open-source simulator — no real
patient's data was ever used to build or test it. See
[regulatory-basis.md](../regulatory-basis.md) for the full basis behind
each of those claims.
